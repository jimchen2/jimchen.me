There is a kind of Chinese train trip you can only take on a slow train: eighteen hours, a hard sleeper berth, middle bunk, the one where you can sit up but not stand. I took one north to Harbin in winter because the high-speed rail would have gotten me there in five boring hours, and I have never once regretted choosing the slow option on purpose.

## Boarding

The station at 9 p.m. is its own country: instant noodle steam, suitcase wheels on tile, a man selling phone chargers with the confidence of a bond trader. I bought sunflower seeds from a vending machine and later understood that this was the correct provisioning decision, because cracking seeds is the national pastime of the hard sleeper car and I was handed into it by osmosis.

My berth neighbors: a grandmother with a whole chicken in a foil bag, two engineering students going home, and a quiet man who unfolded into a person playing chess on his phone at 1 a.m. We shared the chicken. I did not ask why the chicken was there; you do not interrogate the chicken.

## The car at night

- Lights out at 10, but the corridor stays lit, and the corridor is where the conversations happen.
- The window becomes a black mirror with a village passing behind it every few minutes.
- Someone always has tea. Tea appears the way blankets appear. I still do not know from where.
- The rhythm of the rails is exactly loud enough to make your own thoughts feel optional.

I wrote four pages of notes that night and reread them in Harbin: three pages are nonsense and one page has a sentence I have been trying to put in a paper since.

## Harbin, minus twenty

Stepping off the train into minus twenty degrees Celsius is a physical argument. Your eyelashes freeze, your phone dies out of respect, and the city meets you with Russian-domed churches, ice sculptures the size of buses, and 锅包肉 (guo bao rou) that cracks audibly when you bite it. The cold is not weather there, it is architecture; everything in the city assumes it and is built around it, including the people, who are warm in a way southerners are not.

I spent two days walking the frozen Songhua river, where the ice is thick enough for tanks and the locals use it as a park: kites, sleds, a man ice-swimming through a hole he cut himself, waving at us like a neighbor over a fence.

## Why I keep choosing slow travel

The high-speed train optimizes the destination. The slow train keeps the middle, and the middle is where the chicken is, and the chess, and the four pages of notes. Every trip I remember in detail has a night train in it. Every trip I only remember as photos does not.

Next: the return leg, twenty hours, soft sleeper this time as a treat to myself, and a grandmother with a foil bag of something even better.
